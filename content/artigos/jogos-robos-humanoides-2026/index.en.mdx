In 2009, Usain Bolt ran 100 m in 9.58 s and nobody came close for 17 years. On Saturday, 22
August, in Beijing, **a robot covered the distance in 9.39 s** — in an official heat, with a
scoreboard, against two other robots. That same weekend, another one **jumped 2.88 m high from
a standing start** (the human record, with a run-up, is 2.45 m and has stood since 1993); a
third **played tennis against a former world top-15**, served at over 100 km/h, fell flat on
its back and got up on its own; a fourth rallied with an Olympic table tennis champion
**without a single camera on its body**. There were 2,056 robots, 666 teams, 16 countries, five
days — and **97% of all the humanoids shipped in the world this year came out of Chinese
factories.** A year ago, the 100 m winner ran 21.50 s.

The question in the headline is the one the viral posts asked, with 2.75 million views and a
repost from Elon Musk: *is the human era over?*

I wanted to know. I read the 14 most shared posts of the week, downloaded the 11 videos, pulled
them apart frame by frame, read the Chinese press, the fact-checkers and the rulebook — and the
answer has two halves. **The first half is yes: what happened in Beijing is real, it is bigger
than 2025 by an order of magnitude, and it is more impressive than the captions say.** The
second half is what the captions hide: the number Musk reposted is not the number on the
scoreboard; the two most frightening clips on the timeline are fake; and the right question for
each video is not "what time did it run" — it is **"who was in control"**. The robots first.
Then the ruler.

## What actually happened

The 2026 edition ran from 22 to 26 August at the **National Speed Skating Oval** (the "Ice
Ribbon" of the 2022 Winter Olympics). The organiser's numbers, repeated by Xinhua, AP and
Global Times, are consistent with one another:

| | 2025 (1st edition) | 2026 (2nd edition) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Teams | 280 | 666 | +138% |
| Robots | 500+ | 2,056 | ~4× |
| Countries | 16 | 16 | — |
| Events | 26 | 51 (30 competitive + 21 scenario) | ~2× |
| Matches | 487 | 1,301 | +167% |
| 100 m — best official time | 21.50 s | 9.32 s (later preliminary round, via the manufacturer and the state press; 9.39 s on the scoreboard in the viral video) | −12.18 s |

The results that made the headlines, with the source for each:

- **100 m:** Tiangong Ultra (X-Humanoid / Beijing Innovation Center of Humanoid Robotics)
  **9.39 s** in heat 9 of the large group, on the night of 22 August. Honor Lightning **9.47 s**
  in the same heat; Unitree's robot came third and fell after the line
  ([Ifeng](https://news.ifeng.com/c/8vnwlRjYjf5),
  [ZOL](https://ai.zol.com.cn/1235/12358920.html)). In a later preliminary round, that same Tiangong
  Ultra clocked **9.32 s** — a mark claimed by the manufacturer and repeated by the state press
  ([X-Humanoid](https://www.x-humanoid.com/news-view-297.html),
  [北京日报](https://xinwen.bjd.com.cn/content/s6a8a5fdce4b03fa51a83440c.html)). Human record: 9.58 s (Bolt, Berlin, 2009).

## Now the ruler: four numbers, three measurements

The post that carried the news out of China was
[this one, from Trung Phan](https://x.com/TrungTPhan/status/2091183427303383137), 2.75 million
views, reposted by Elon Musk: *"Chinese humanoid robot by smartphone maker Honor ('Lightning')
ran 100m in 9.32 seconds."*

I downloaded the video and pulled the frames. **At 18 seconds, the stadium scoreboard reads
"大型组100米预赛9组 — 9.39"**: large group, 100 m, heat 9, nine point three nine. The robot that
won that heat is not Honor's Lightning — it is the one from **team Tianzhuo (天卓队)**, from
Beijing's humanoid innovation centre, which the Chinese press identifies as the **Tiangong
Ultra**. According to that same press, Lightning led off the gun, was passed near the line and
ran **9.47 s**. The "9.32" in the caption does exist, but it came from a **preparatory test
event**, outside competition, reported by Chinese state TV
([IT之家](https://m.ithome.com/html/992981.htm)). And there is a second 9.32, this one official:
the Tiangong Ultra clocked the same mark in a **later preliminary round** of the 100 m — claimed by
the [manufacturer](https://www.x-humanoid.com/news-view-297.html) and repeated by the state press
([北京日报](https://xinwen.bjd.com.cn/content/s6a8a5fdce4b03fa51a83440c.html),
[中新网](https://www.chinanews.com.cn/sh/2026/08-23/10682731.shtml)). Four numbers, three rulers, one
caption — and two of the numbers are the same 9.32.

That does not cancel the feat — **9.39 s in an official heat is 12 seconds faster than the 2025
winner**. It cancels the caption. The 9.32 s that made the headlines is not the same kind of
number as Bolt's 9.58 s — nor the same kind as the 9.39 s of the heat, nor the same 9.32 s that the
Tiangong Ultra went on to run in official competition. The numerical coincidence invites
confusion (the *Yomiuri* appears to have fused the two marks into a single sentence). Worth
separating them:

Two details the English-language coverage skipped over:

**Lightning grew between one event and the next.** At April's half marathon, the robot was 169 cm
tall with 95 cm legs; for the Games, Honor **lengthened the legs by 10 cm**
([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2026/aug/22/chinese-robot-runs-100m-sprint-quicker-usain-bolt-world-record)).
It is not cheating — it is engineering, and it is exactly why "record" is the wrong word: **the
athlete was redesigned for the event.** A running blog summed it up well: Lightning "appears to
have stumbled into the *Enhanced Games* category"
([the5krunner](https://the5krunner.com/2026/08/22/honor-robot-100m-record/)).

**Neither of them managed to stop.** After the line, Tiangong Ultra staggered to the edge of the
track and the crowd got out of the way; Lightning hit the crash mat, went down and **left on a
stretcher** (ZOL). The stretcher is in the last 15 seconds of Trung Phan's own video, with four
technicians carrying a robot — which of the two, the video does not let you tell.

## The autonomy ruler — the number that matters and nobody puts in the caption

The right question for every clip is not "what time did it run", it is **"who was in control"**.
The Games have an official answer, and it differs from event to event:

- **In the flat 100 m there is no remote control.** This edition's rulebook requires every track
  event to be completed by the robot fully autonomously (全自主), with an exception only for the
  100 m and the 400 m **obstacle races**
  ([CCTV, via Tencent](https://news.qq.com/rain/a/20260813A05R5800), 13 Aug;
  [新京报](https://www.bjnews.com.cn/detail/1786599722129195.html), 13 Aug;
  [Beijing municipal government](https://english.beijing.gov.cn/latest/news/202608/t20260815_4824032.html), 15 Aug).
  The heat in which Tiangong Ultra ran 9.39 s, therefore, ran with no operator: the robots navigate
  by their own perception, with nobody at the controls — an inference from the rule, not a claim by
  any source about that particular heat. Xinhua's list ([12 Jun](https://www.news.cn/tech/20260612/dcb73df4dc7949a3a7aeaffe9017d04e/c.html),
  [22 Aug](https://www.news.cn/tech/20260822/1f62f563c72241cbae252030caeabc5d/c.html)) —
  **the 400 m, the 1,500 m and the 4×100 m relay "upgraded to fully autonomous"** in this edition —
  does not mention the 100 m because they were already autonomous and needed no *upgrade*.
- In the **scenario events** (factory, hotel, home, rescue), the organiser penalises teleoperation
  through the score: autonomous mode is worth 100% of the points, **teleoperated mode is worth at
  most 50% per task** ([Sohu](https://m.sohu.com/a/1066414039_122014422)).
- At **April's half marathon**, the ruler was a multiplier: teleoperated robots had their time
  multiplied by 1.2. The autonomous Lightning ran 50:26 (human record: 57:20, Kiplimo); a
  teleoperated version of the same model ran 48:19 raw and finished behind once adjusted
  ([iRunFar](https://www.irunfar.com/2026-beijing-e-town-half-marathon-humanoid-robot-beats-human-world-record)).
  In that same race, **only 38% of the robots ran autonomously**
  ([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/world-humanoid-robot-games-beijing-usain-bolt-record)).

Table tennis is the most honest case: the organiser *says* the A3 played "free of remote
controls, pre-written scripts or live human input" — and says, in the same piece, that its eyes
are 10 cameras fixed to the ceiling. It is decision autonomy with external perception.
Impressive, and not transferable to a factory without an instrumented ceiling.

That is the ruler that separates a demo from a product. And it is what the event itself is
measuring: the 21 new scenario events — weighing powder, picking up beans with tweezers, opening
a bottle, shutting off a valve, working a fire extinguisher — exist because **running in a
straight line on a blue track is the easy problem**. Beijing's economy director, Jiang Guangzhi,
said at the opening: "every extra centimetre in the jump, every extra kilogram in the lift, is
the result of engineers optimising joint motors and reducers" — the event is an actuator test
bench, not an Olympics.

## The work shift: the thesis the viral posts argue, and what holds it up

The two long pieces that travelled with the clips — ["The Human Era Is Ending: Robots Are
Taking the Shift"](https://x.com/will_i__amm/status/2090074790786715759) (199,000 views) and
["They stopped arguing about whether robots would show up. The robots already
did"](https://x.com/OxVelnox/status/2087100097192509731) (1.37 million views, from an account
with 359 followers) — argue the same thesis: **the demo era is over, humanoids are already
working shifts.** Neither of them cites a primary source. But the thesis has evidence of its own,
and it is better than the essays:

- **Volume.** In the first half of 2026, **around 19,100 humanoids** were shipped worldwide, 3×
  the same period in 2025. **97% came from Chinese makers**: AgiBot ~8,400 (44%), Unitree ~5,900
  (31%) — the two together are three quarters of the market (Smart Analytics Global, via
  [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2026-08-10/china-humanoid-makers-hold-97-of-global-shipments-report-says)
  and [Forbes](https://www.forbes.com/sites/johnkoetsier/2026/08/20/humanoid-robot-shipments-up-300-up-to-30000-so-far-in-2026/)).
  Tesla had shipped ~150 Optimus by January
  ([24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/08/24/elon-musk-admits-china-is-by-far-strongest-ai-competitor-as-chinese-robot-just-beat-usain-bolts-100m-world-record/)).
- **Policy.** In July, the US FCC **banned imports of new foreign-made humanoids** citing national
  security — the target is China
  ([Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/sport/2026/aug/22/chinese-robot-runs-100m-sprint-quicker-usain-bolt-world-record)).
  Three days before the Games, Musk wrote that China is "also by far the strongest competitor in
  AI"; back in January, on Tesla's earnings call, he had already said that "by far the biggest
  competition in humanoid robots will come from China".
- **What the robots do when they are not running.** The scenario events are the portrait: 20+
  teams on the rescue circuit (identify hazardous material, close a valve, work a fire
  extinguisher, in 30 min); a team from Shanghai completed the circuit **using a hook so it would
  not have to hold the extinguisher** ([Sohu](https://m.sohu.com/a/1066414039_122014422)). That —
  the grasping workaround — is what shows where the frontier is.

And there are people who disagree with the whole framing, with credentials. Rodney Brooks (MIT,
founder of iRobot) called the earlier race a "publicity stunt… it shows no safety at all";
Yanran Ding (Michigan) points out that people have "a cognitive bias to think that running a half
marathon faster than a human is more difficult than folding laundry"; Alan Fern (Oregon State)
puts the leap down to "engineering and investment rather than a breakthrough"
([TNW](https://thenextweb.com/news/world-humanoid-robot-games-beijing-usain-bolt-record)).
All three are right and none of them contradicts the 19,100 shipments. **Running fast and working
a shift are two different curves, and the Games measure the first one.**

## What I would do with this

- **If you write about robotics:** before reposting a time, look for the scoreboard in the video.
  The one from 22 August was full-screen for two seconds. Heat ≠ final ≠ test; and "autonomous"
  only counts if the event's rulebook says so.
- **If you invest or buy:** the number of the week is not 9.32 — it is **97% of shipments**. If
  your supply chain depends on a humanoid and your supplier is not Chinese, you are buying from
  3% of the market, under an FCC rule that changes the rest.
- **If you build agents (my own case):** the A3 with no camera on board is the lesson. The robot's
  "autonomy" was real and the perception belonged to the arena. When a vendor says "fully
  autonomous", ask where the sensors are — and who installed them.
- **If you just want to watch the videos:** the Galbot tennis one is the one worth 43 seconds of
  your life. The 100 m one is worth it for the last 15.

## What is fake in this timeline — and what is actually happening

Of the 14 posts I analysed, two are fabrications and one has the wrong caption. What each one
claims, what it is, and how it checks out:

**1. "Shooting test of the Unitree robots — in the future, wars will not need humans"**
([@Eng_china5](https://x.com/Eng_china5/status/2023933070735536501), 18 February 2026, **2.09
million views**). **AI-generated.** The video was made by user *oukanghong* on Bilibili on 17
February with the Seedance 2.0 model, **with a red-text warning that the shooting scenes were
fictional** — the warning vanished on the reposts. Tells: shells do not eject, an empty magazine
goes in at 0:23, the logo on the chest disappears and comes back, a robot walks through sandbags
like a ghost. Checked by
[BOOM](https://www.boomlive.in/fact-check/viral-video-chinese-company-unitree-robots-war-preparation-weapons-claim-fact-check-30710)
(19 February), [DW, via Newswire](https://www.newswire.lk/2026/02/23/fact-check-are-chinas-robot-soldiers-just-ai-fakes/)
and a Community Note on the post itself. **What is real:** Unitree's G1 robots exist, they danced
at the Chinese New Year Gala — and that is where the creator took his inspiration from.

**2. "A humanoid rallied a shuttlecock against a human in Beijing. No script."**
([@OxVelnox](https://x.com/OxVelnox/status/2091466868036890670), 23 August 2026, **708,000
views**). **Manipulated video, and an old one.** The original is a Vietnamese badminton TikTok,
posted on **12 December 2023**, with three human players; someone replaced one of them with a
robot — the court, the shadows, the sound and the movements are identical to the original. Taken
apart by D-Intent Data on 30 December 2023 and by
[Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/fact-check/video-robot-badminton/) ("Fake"). The Community Note is
on the post; the text ("Track. Predict. Reposition. Swing.") is a good paragraph about a video
that does not exist. **What is real:** Galbot's tennis against Zheng Jie, the same weekend, does
exactly what the post describes — and it has a source.

**3. "Lightning ran 100 m in 9.32 s"** ([@TrungTPhan](https://x.com/TrungTPhan/status/2091183427303383137),
2.75 million views, reposted by Musk). **Real video, wrong caption.** The scoreboard in the video
reads 9.39, from heat 9; the 9.32 in the caption is the one from Honor's test, outside competition
(the Tiangong Ultra clocked an official 9.32 in a later preliminary round — another number with the
same digits); Lightning ran 9.47 in that race. It is not disinformation — it is three numbers fused
into one, which is how most records are going to travel from now on.

**I could not verify:** the 12-second clip in [Evan Kirstel's reply](https://x.com/EvanKirstel/status/2091212428973367575)
shows a robot down on the ground throwing sparks with a technician beside it — it is the scene
Trung refers to ("it crashed into a safety wall without having its waist implode with sparks
flying off"). The track is the same; the robot and the heat, I could not identify.

## Open questions

Three points the first version of this piece left open. Two of them closed on 24 August, with
targeted research in Chinese, English, German and Japanese; one is still open:

1. **The 100 m autonomy rule — closed on 24 August.** This edition's rulebook requires every track
   event to be fully autonomous (全自主); the exception is only for the 100 m and 400 m obstacle
   races (CCTV, 新京报, Beijing municipal government). The 9.39 s heat ran with no operator (detail
   in the section "The autonomy ruler").
2. **"Caught fire" — closed on 24 August, with a caveat.** Two independent sources (a Reuters photo
   caption in the *Irish Times*; the *Yomiuri*) confirm that a robot fell and caught fire after the
   finish of the 100 m preliminaries. Which robot, no source names with any confidence — this piece
   does not name it (detail and frame in the section "Now the ruler").
3. **The 100 m final has not been run yet.** According to the organisers' schedule
   ([澎湃新闻](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_33833753)), the repechage is on the night of
   25 August and the final on the night of 26 August, in Beijing. The final decided on 24 August was
   the **100 m obstacle race** (team Zhiyuan of Shenzhen, 1:28.32). This article will be updated with
   the result of the final — update expected after 26 August 2026.

- The 14 posts were read through X's public API (metadata, views, Community Notes) on 24 August

  (9.39) is the central evidence of this piece and it is in the dossier. **What the frame

  start line is which — the attribution Tiangong Ultra 9.39 / Lightning 9.47 / Unitree 3rd comes

  from the Beijing Innovation Center of Humanoid Robotics (X-Humanoid). The English-language
  coverage uses now one, now the other, as if they were two different runners.

  "40,000+" cited by Xinhua from a Chinese industry report. I used the smaller figure, the one
  with an identified source; the two agree on the 97%.
- No comparison with Bolt's record in this piece is an athletic comparison: the rulers are
  different by construction (starting blocks, reaction time, wind, anti-doping on one side;
  lengthened legs and a stretcher on the other).
