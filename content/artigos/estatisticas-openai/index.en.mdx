The most-repeated number about OpenAI in 2026 — "92% of Fortune 500 companies use our
products" — is 31 months old and came from a post about a lawsuit. The line appears in a
piece from **January 8, 2024**, the company's response to the New York Times lawsuit; statistics
roundups hang it on the "1 million business customers" announcement from November 2025, which
**does not contain the number**. I read both. OpenAI never updated the percentage.

I went after the provenance of every statistic circulating about the most-cited and
least-documented company in the industry. I classified the 20 core numbers into four natures —
**official, reporting, target, and orphan** — and the ruler reorganizes the whole subject: only
8 come from OpenAI or public record; 6 are press reporting; 3 are intent; and 3 have no
traceable origin. The only audited document that exists about the company is a tax filing from
the original foundation, and it tells a story no one cites.

> **Methodological note.** Every number was checked against the original source on August 26,
> 2026 — an official post, a funding-round announcement, a Form 990 filed with the IRS (via
> ProPublica), the SEC's EDGAR database, a pricing page — or, when the source is press reporting,
> against the reporting that originated it. OpenAI's website blocks automated reading (a 403
> error on every page); anything from there was read in an archived copy on the Wayback Machine
> or in the browser, and the text says which. Three measurements are my own and reproducible: the
> two Form 990 filings from "OpenAI Inc" (FY2023 and FY2024), the search for the company's
> records at the SEC, and the pricing page's history on the Wayback Machine. ChatGPT users are
> out of scope — I already covered them [here](/artigos/estatisticas-chatgpt). There is no
> Reddit or X; the Hacker News discussion of these numbers is thin, and I say where.

(the target, the leak, the "92%") is exactly what the company never signed off on.

## The ruler: four questions before repeating a number

Before believing any statistic about a closely held company, one question separates what the
number proves from what it only suggests: **who signed it?** The figure is the whole ruler; the
rest of the article is the demonstration, number by number.

/>

Notice that the ruler doesn't say "official is true, the rest is a lie." It says every box
carries a weight: the official can be checked at the source, with a date — and it can be stale;
the reporting is only as good as the digging behind it, and it changes when that changes; the
target is intent with a deadline, not fact; and the orphan carries no weight at all, because
there's nothing to check. The roundups' mistake isn't inventing numbers. It's putting all four
boxes in the same sentence, with the same verb.

The German page is a good case precisely because it's better than average: it names its
sources. The problem is what's left when you pull on each link.

## The "92%": a 2024 number about a lawsuit, recycled with no date

The official post from November 5, 2025, *"1 million business customers: the fastest-growing
business platform in history,"* says what it says — and only that:

> "Today, we're announcing that more than 1 million business customers around the world are
> directly using OpenAI [...]. We now have more than 7 million total ChatGPT for Work seats (up
> 40% in just 2 months). ChatGPT Enterprise seats specifically have grown 9x year-over-year."

I read the entire text, not a word search. There's no "92%," no "Fortune 500," no "460
companies," no chart comparing OpenAI with Google, Microsoft, and Anthropic, no "600,000"
organizations, "2.2 billion" calls, "3,400" apps, or "48%" share — the six numbers the German
page lists under the single label *"Source: OpenAI, 1 Million Businesses,"* next to a chart it
draws itself.

The "92%" is real. It's in a post from **January 8, 2024**, titled *"OpenAI and journalism,"*
the company's response to the New York Times' copyright lawsuit:

> "Millions of developers and more than 92% of Fortune 500 are building on our products today."

"Today" was January 2024 — thirteen months after ChatGPT launched, in a paragraph of legal
defense. OpenAI never republished the updated percentage; the November 2025 announcement, which
would be the natural place for it, doesn't mention it. **Every piece that says "92% of the
Fortune 500" citing OpenAI in 2026 is using a number from 31 months ago, about a different
subject, with no date attached.** It's the "official" nature past its expiration date — and
that's why it stays out of the table's 20: it's not an orphan, it's a fossil.

The three orphans are a different case. I searched for "2.2 billion API calls per day" in five
targeted searches and found no OpenAI publication, consultancy, or report with that number. What
officially exists is a different metric in a different unit — *"Our APIs now process more than
15 billion tokens per minute"* (tokens being the chunks of text the model reads and produces), in
the March 2026 post. "3,400 education apps" and "48% of real-time customer-service bots" don't
appear anywhere outside roundups that cite each other. **A number whose cited source doesn't
contain it isn't imprecise: it's nonexistent until proven otherwise.**

## Revenue: what OpenAI said, what leaked, what's a target

Revenue is the subject where the four natures blur together most in a single sentence: "OpenAI
makes US$ 20 billion, will close 2026 at 30, and is aiming for 100 in 2027." Separated, the
three parts carry different weights.

<CountryBarsChart
  dataset="openai-receita-por-natureza-en"
  title="The same revenue line, three natures: 24 official, 40 estimate, 280 target"
  subtitle="US$ billions, annualized — each block shows who signed it; never add them up or draw a line between blocks"
  source="Sources: OpenAI (Jan 2026; post 03/31/2026) · Sacra (08/26/2026) · The Information · BG2 · Bloomberg. ulissesflores.com/openai-en"
  description="Three blocks of horizontal bars showing annualized revenue in billions of dollars. Block 2, gray, outside estimate: July 2026, 40, per Sacra's live dashboard read on August 26. Block 3, amber, targets: 2026, 30; 2027, 100, said on a podcast; 2030, 280."
/>

Notice the blue block: it's what the company signed off on, and it's consistent. CFO Sarah
Friar wrote on the official blog, in January 2026, *"$2B ARR in 2023, $6B in 2024, and $20B+ in
2025."* On March 31, the post announcing the US$ 122bn round said, in text — I read it in the
archived HTML of the page itself:

> "Within a year of launching ChatGPT, we reached $1B in revenue. By the end of 2024 we were
> generating $1B per quarter. We are now generating $2B in revenue per month."

Two billion a month is US$ 24bn a year — an official, dated number, and the only one in the
block that describes the present. From there, everything is external. The gray block is Sacra,
a consultancy that keeps a live dashboard on the company: on August 26 it read *"OpenAI hit $40B
in annualized revenue in July 2026."* The German page cites Sacra at "25bn in February" — a
number the dashboard **no longer shows**, because the consultancy updates the estimate and
erases the previous one. A live-dashboard estimate has to be cited with the date it was read,
not the date of the number.

The amber block is intent. "2026 target: 29.4bn" doesn't exist in any source with that decimal
figure; what The Information published, based on internal documents, is **US$ 30bn** — the
"29.4" only shows up in roundups that cite each other. "100bn in 2027" is Altman's answer to
investor Brad Gerstner, on the BG2 podcast, in October 2025: Gerstner suggested 2028 or 2029 and
Altman shot back *"How about '27?"* The German page writes "hinted by Altman," which is correct
— and it's exactly the weight a line from a podcast carries. "280bn in 2030" comes from internal
documents Bloomberg saw in February. I already wrote here [about how the "900 million weekly
users" came from the same funding-round post](/artigos/estatisticas-chatgpt), with no
methodological note: the official revenue is the only line in that post that comes with a unit
and a period.

**Twenty-four is official, forty is an estimate, two hundred eighty is a target — and the
sentence that lumps the three together with the same verb is wrong even if every number is
right.**

## Fundraising: the rounds, and the difference between investing and pledging compute

OpenAI is the company that has raised the most private money in history, and roundups usually
get the round right and the cumulative total wrong — because they add up things of different
natures.

Notice the first point: the "US$ 1bn" from 2015 was a pledge from the founders, and CNBC found
that only **US$ 130 million** had actually come in by 2019. The last point, by contrast, is
official and unambiguous: *"$122 billion in committed capital at a post money valuation of $852

US$ 730bn "pre-money" (the valuation before the new capital comes in) closed bigger. Three
months later, in May, Anthropic announced its own round at US$ 965bn and pulled ahead; the
German page records that correctly.

The mistake is in the cumulative total per investor.

Notice the first bar. The German page says SoftBank invested "more than US$ 71bn." SoftBank's
own release, from February 27, 2026, says:

> "Upon completion of the Follow-on Investment, SBG's cumulative investment in OpenAI is
> expected to total USD 64.6 billion, representing an ownership interest of approximately 13%."

That's US$ 34.6bn already invested since September 2024 plus US$ 30bn in three equal
installments through 2026 (April, July, October). I didn't find "71" in any source, primary or
secondary; Forbes, in June, put it at "US$ 65bn for 13%." And note that SoftBank's bar already
includes money that, as of this article's date, hasn't come in yet — the October installment.

Now the second block, which is where the inflated cumulative totals come from. Amazon invested
US$ 50bn (US$ 35bn conditional on an IPO or a technical milestone; released in July) — and,
separately, expanded a compute contract with AWS by **US$ 100bn over eight years**. Nvidia
invested US$ 30bn — and signed, in September 2025, a letter of intent for up to US$ 100bn
"progressively, as each gigawatt gets installed," which Nvidia's own CFO, Colette Kress,
described in November as *"still haven't completed a definitive agreement."* And Stargate, the
US$ 500bn in data centers with SoftBank and Oracle, was announced on **January 21, 2025** — the
page says 2026 — and is an infrastructure commitment, not capital in the company. **Adding 50 to
100, or 30 to "up to 100," produces a number nobody invested.** The historical cumulative "of
~US$ 190bn" that circulates is an aggregator's sum: sources that try to add it up land between
US$ 180bn and US$ 190.6bn, depending on whether secondary sales and credit lines are counted, and
none of them is OpenAI.

One last check, which is my own measurement: I searched the SEC's public database, EDGAR, for
the company's records. A search for "openai" returns 24 entities — OpenAI's own investment fund
for startups, third-party vehicles that package its shares for resale, and an Illinois aviation
company called Openairplane. **None of the filings is operating OpenAI**: 46 Form D filings
mention the name, zero list "OpenAI Global, LLC" as the issuer. That doesn't prove wrongdoing —
there are legal structures that waive direct-name registration, and June's IPO filing is
confidential by law until it goes public. It proves something else: for the most valuable
company in the industry, the only audited document available to anyone is the tax filing of the
nonprofit foundation that controls it. That's the next section.

## The one audited document: the Form 990, and the story no one cites

Every nonprofit in the United States files a Form 990 with the tax authority, and it's public.
"OpenAI Inc" — the original 2015 entity, which by the 2024 filings already identifies itself as
`openai.com/foundation` — files its own, and that's where the one audited number that
circulates about the company comes from: "Altman's salary: US$ 76,001." I downloaded the two
most recent filings from ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer.

  dataset="openai-form990-remuneracao-en"

  subtitle="OpenAI Inc's Form 990 (EIN 81-0861541), Part VII, columns E + F; the column paid by the foundation is zero"

Block 2, gray, the highest-paid people in the same 2024 filing: Greg Brockman, former foundation president, 264,189; Chris Clark, treasurer and secretary, 216,021; Lawrence Summers, board member, 143,702; Bret Taylor, board chair, 140,000."
/>

Notice that the number matches to the cent — and it's the **sum of two columns**. In Part VII of
the 2023 filing, Altman's line carries three figures: column D, "compensation from the
organization itself," **0**; column E, "compensation from related organizations," **64,957**;
column F, "other estimated compensation," **11,044**. The foundation pays him nothing; the sum
that circulates as "salary" comes from related entities — the for-profit arm — and estimated
benefits. The 2024 filing, submitted in November 2025 and cited by no roundup, shows **0 +
65,638 + 48,036 = US$ 113,674**, 49% more in a single year, almost all of it in the "other
compensation" column. And that year's Schedule J adds, in text, that three listed people took
part in *"an equity-based compensation arrangement consisting of profits interest issued by a
related organization"* (profits interest meaning a right to future profits), with zero grant-date
settlement value — meaning there's compensation the number doesn't capture.

What no one cites is in the second block: **in the 2024 filing, the CEO is tenth of twelve names
on the list.** I extracted all of Part VII. Above him: Greg Brockman, listed as *"Dir/Pres
(Former),"* US$ 264,189; Chris Clark, treasurer and secretary, US$ 216,021; Lawrence Summers,
US$ 143,702; board members Adam D'Angelo, Susan Desmond-Hellmann, and Bret Taylor, US$ 140,000
each; Nicole Seligman and Fidji Simo, US$ 127,500; and Ilya Sutskever, already listed as
*"Director (Former),"* US$ 119,538 — he left in 2024 to found SSI and still shows up receiving
pay from a related organization. Below Altman, only Paul Nakasone (US$ 99,030) and Zico Kolter
(US$ 53,494). The 2023 filing has another oddity: a line reading *"Emmett Shear — CEO — 40.00
[hours] — 3,720"* — the interim CEO of the few days in November 2023, with US$ 3,720, is the
most concrete public record that episode has.

And the foundation's own revenue: **US$ 5.36 million** in 2023, **US$ 691 thousand** in 2024.
That's not the company's revenue — it's the nonprofit entity's, which brings in directly less
and less while the billions run through the subsidiary. Comparing that number with the "US$
20bn" is the inverse mistake of the "92%": the nature is official and audited, and the object is
a different one.

**The Form 990 is the one number about OpenAI that comes with an auditor's signature — and
every roundup cites it wrong on the label, the year, and what it measures.**

## Pricing: the "US$ 100 Pro" that never existed and the plan the page missed

Price is the easiest statistic to check and the one that changes the most. The German page
lists ChatGPT's plans as "Free; Plus US$ 20; Pro US$ 100 or 200" — and the "or" already gives away the
confusion. I went to the history.

In the archived copies of the official pricing page, from January 2024 to August 2025, Pro
always cost **US$ 200 a month** (the plan didn't even exist in January 2024; Plus cost US$ 20,
same as today). There was never a "US$ 100 Pro" in that stretch. On the live page, read in the
browser on August 26, 2026, there are four individual plans — Free, **Go at US$ 8**, Plus at
US$ 20, and Pro *"From $100"* — and Go, the mid-tier plan launched in 2025, doesn't appear on the
German page. The likely origin of "100 or 200" is the business tab, where the "Premium" seat
costs US$ 100 a month on the annual plan and US$ 125 monthly. **The price changed between the
last archived copy and today, and the Wayback Machine has no readable capture of OpenAI's
pricing page since June 11, 2026**: 39 attempts by the Internet Archive's crawler, all blocked.
The most-cited company in the industry has a two-and-a-half-month hole in its public pricing
history, opened by the company itself.

In the API, the same pattern: the flagship model, GPT-5.6 Sol, shows a promotional price (US$ 4
per million input tokens, "was $5"; US$ 20 output, "was $30") valid "at least through November
21, 2026" — and its siblings Terra and Luna also dropped since their July launch, with no mark
on the page showing the change. A price number with no date is an editorial choice.

## What I'd do with this

One question, before repeating any number about OpenAI — or about any closely held company:
**who signed it?**

1. **If the company signed it**, check the date. The "92%" is official and is 31 months old.
   The "76,001" is audited and is from 2023; 2024's comes to 113,674. Stale official is the
   most respectable way to be wrong.
2. **If it was reporting**, cite the reporting, not the company — and cite the date you read it
   when the source is a dashboard that updates itself. Sacra's February figure said 25;
   August's says 40.
3. **If it's a target**, use the right verb: "projects," "said it intends to," "answered on a
   podcast." A target with a decimal figure ("29.4") is a sign someone copied someone else.
4. **If the cited source doesn't contain the number**, the number doesn't exist. It's not
   "approximate," it's not an "estimate": it's an orphan, and orphans don't go in the text.
